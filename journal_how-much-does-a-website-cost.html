A website in 2026 can cost nothing, or it can cost more than a car. A free builder page, a freelancer, a studio and a large agency will all sell you something called a website, and the prices climb steeply at each step. Our own number, so you have one real figure to hold on to: a custom five-page website from UNLEESH starts at $499. The rest of this entry explains why quotes vary so much and how to tell which price is right for you.

What decides the price of a website?

Five things move the price. Page count is the obvious one. The second is whether the design is custom or a template with your logo dropped in. The third is content: who writes the words and where the images come from. The fourth is function, meaning anything the site has to do beyond being read, such as booking, payments, logins or a product catalogue. The fifth is what happens after launch: search optimization, content and upkeep. Most surprise invoices come from numbers three and five, because nobody priced them at the start.

A cheap website is not the one with the lowest invoice. It is the one that pays for itself fastest.

What do the common options cost you?

The cheapest route is a do-it-yourself builder. It costs a monthly fee and your own evenings. That is a fair trade for a brand-new business that needs to exist online this week. The hidden cost shows up later: a template that looks like a thousand other sites, pages that load slowly, and a platform you rent rather than own.

A freelancer sits between the builder and the agency. You get one person's skill and one person's availability. That works well when the person is good and stays reachable. Ask who maintains the site if they move on, and whether you will hold the logins.

The most expensive route is a large agency. You are paying for account managers, discovery workshops and a process built for companies with a marketing department. If you have one, that process is worth it. If you are an owner who needs a site that brings in enquiries, you are mostly paying for meetings.

The middle is where most small businesses should shop, and it is where the quality varies most. Ask three questions of any quote. Is the price fixed, or an estimate? Do I own the code and the domain when it is done? And what exactly is included for search: page titles, structured data, speed, a sitemap, or nothing? A site nobody can find is the most expensive kind, whatever the invoice says.

What are the costs nobody mentions?

Four costs sit outside most quotes. A domain name, renewed every year. Hosting, paid monthly or yearly. Email on your own domain. And changes after launch: new pages, new photos, a new offer. None of them is large. All of them are easier to plan for when they are written down at the start. Ask every provider which of the four are in the price and which are not.

How do you know what you should pay?

A cheap website is not the one with the lowest invoice. It is the one that pays for itself fastest. Price the site against what one new customer is worth to you, then ask how many customers it needs to bring in to cover its cost. If the answer is one or two, the decision is easier than it looked.

Then compare quotes on the same five lines: page count, custom or template, who writes the copy, what the site has to do, and what happens after launch. Two quotes that look far apart often turn out to be pricing different jobs. The useful question is not which is cheaper. It is which one includes the things that make the site earn its keep.

Quick answers

Can you get a website for free?

Yes, on a builder's free plan, usually with the builder's branding and web address instead of your own. It is fine for testing an idea. It is a weak first impression for a business asking people to pay.

Is a $499 website any good?

It depends what the $499 buys. Ours buys five custom, hand-coded pages with search foundations built in. The price is low because the build takes days instead of weeks, not because corners are cut.

What should a small business budget for a website?

Budget for the build, then for the four running costs above, then for the work that gets the site found. A site with no plan for search or content tends to sit unvisited, whatever it cost to make.
