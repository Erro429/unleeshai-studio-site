Open ten agency sites in ten tabs and read only the first viewport. You will find the same sentence wearing different fonts: we craft digital experiences that drive results. Nobody hired anyone because of that sentence. It exists because a committee could not agree on what to leave out, so it left nothing out.

A hero is not a summary of the company. It is a single decision about what the visitor should feel first. On a fine-dining site that feeling is patience; on a machining site it is precision; on a skincare flagship it is calm. Once the feeling is chosen, the hero needs one image or loop that produces it, one line of type that names it, and a way down. Everything else belongs further down the page where the visitor has already decided to stay.

A hero is not a summary of the company. It is a single decision about what the visitor should feel first.

The cost of getting this wrong is measurable. Picture a B2B hero where the headline, a stat ticker, a video, a client logo strip and two buttons all compete for the same second. Paid traffic lands, finds nothing to hold on to, and leaves before the first scroll. Remove four of the six elements and the page finally says one thing, without a dollar more in ad spend.

The uncomfortable part is that the fix requires someone to be wrong. The stat ticker was somebody's idea. The logo strip was a sales request. Subtraction is a political act inside a company, which is why it is worth paying an outside studio to perform it.
