WebGL is the difference between a site that plays a film and a site that renders one. That distinction is powerful and mostly unnecessary. If a video would do the same job, use the video: it is smaller, it works everywhere, and nobody's laptop fan will spin up.

WebGL earns its place when the reader's input changes the picture in a way a video cannot anticipate: a product that turns to follow the cursor, an image that ripples where you touch it, a field of points that parts as you scroll through it. The moment the picture is interactive, the shader stops being decoration.

If a video would do the same job, use the video.

It also has to survive contact with the real world. Every WebGL layer we ship has a mobile fallback, disposes its own memory, and stops rendering when it leaves the viewport. A hero that is beautiful on a studio Mac and molten on a two-year-old phone is a failure dressed as an achievement.

The question to ask is not whether the effect is impressive. It is whether the reader would notice if it were gone. If the answer is no, it was vanity, and vanity is the one thing a premium brand cannot afford to be caught doing.
