A sales funnel is the path a stranger takes to become a customer, mapped out and built on purpose. In practice it is a short chain of pages and messages: something that gets attention, a page that makes one offer, a way to act on it, and follow-up for the people who are not ready yet. Yes, a small business needs one, because it already has one. Every business has a funnel. Most of them were built by accident.

What does an accidental funnel look like?

The accidental version looks like this. Someone hears about you, searches your name, lands on a home page, hunts for a phone number, and maybe calls. Each of those steps loses people. A funnel is what you get when you look at each step and ask what would stop someone here.

Every business has a funnel. Most of them were built by accident.

What are the parts of a simple funnel?

A simple funnel has four parts. Traffic: search, ads, social posts or referrals that bring the right people in. A landing page: one offer, one action, no distractions. A capture: a form, a booking calendar or a checkout. And follow-up: an email or message sequence that answers the questions people have before they buy.

Why does the offer matter most?

The offer matters more than the pages. A free quote, a guide, a consultation, a first-order discount: whatever it is, it has to be worth giving an email address or a phone number for. A beautiful page with a weak offer converts worse than a plain page with a strong one.

Why is follow-up where the money is?

Follow-up is where most of the money is. Few people buy on the first visit. A funnel without follow-up pays to attract visitors and then lets nearly all of them leave with no way to reach them again. Three or four honest emails that answer real objections will often do more than another month of ad spend.

How do you measure a funnel?

Measure each step separately. How many people arrive, how many act on the offer, how many respond to follow-up, how many become customers. The step with the biggest drop is the one to fix first. Without those four numbers, every change is a guess.

What does a small-business funnel look like?

Take a home services company. An ad offers a free quote for one specific job. The landing page repeats that offer and asks for a name, a phone number and a postcode. The thank-you page says when to expect a call. Two emails follow for anyone who does not pick up: one explaining what the job usually involves, one answering what past customers asked first. Four steps, and each one can be measured.
